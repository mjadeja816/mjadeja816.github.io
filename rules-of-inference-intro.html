Discrete Mathematics

Rules of Inference: A Problem-Solving Starter

The central habit is simple: translate statements into symbols, match the left-hand side of a valid rule, then write the conclusion with the rule name and statement numbers.

Study the examples More discrete math demos

Core Template

S
Premises are assumed trueExample: P -> Q, P
R
Match a known rule patternExample: Modus Ponens
C
Derive only what followsExample: conclude Q
1. Name atoms

Choose variables for simple statements: P for raining, Q for coffee, and so on.

2. Translate carefully

Convert English into connectives: implication, conjunction, disjunction, and negation.

3. Match templates

Look for the exact premise shape required by a rule such as Modus Ponens or Transitivity.

4. Justify each line

Write the rule name and the earlier statement numbers used to obtain the new line.

Three Concrete Examples

From English statements to valid conclusions

These examples are based on the classroom flow in the two rules-of-inference transcripts: identify propositions, convert the argument, and decide whether the conclusion is valid.

Example 1

Guilty implies punished

Modus Ponens Valid
  • P
    P: I am guilty.
  • Q
    Q: I must be punished.
  • 1
    If I am guilty, then I must be punished. P -> Q
  • 2
    I am guilty. P
3
Q, from 1 and 2 by Modus Ponens.

When both P -> Q and P are available, the conclusion Q follows directly.

Example 2

Not guilty does not imply not punished

Invalid argument Counterexample
  • P
    P: I am guilty.
  • Q
    Q: I must be punished.
  • 1
    If I am guilty, then I must be punished. P -> Q
  • 2
    I am not guilty. not P
  • C
    Therefore I must not be punished. not Q
P = false
not P = true
Q = true
not Q = false

The premises can be true while the conclusion is false, so the argument is not valid.

Example 3

Raining, party tonight, party tomorrow

Transitivity Valid
  • P
    P: It rains today.
  • Q
    Q: We have a party tonight.
  • R
    R: We have a party tomorrow.
  • 1
    If it rains today, then no party tonight. P -> not Q
  • 2
    If no party tonight, then party tomorrow. not Q -> R
3
P -> R, from 1 and 2 by Transitivity.

If the conclusion of one implication is the premise of the next, chain them and remove the middle statement.

Effective Use

What students should write in solutions

A proof should read like a sequence of accountable moves. Number the given statements, derive one new statement at a time, and mention the rule used in parentheses. If the argument is invalid, give one truth assignment where every premise is true and the conclusion is false.

Rule Pattern Problem-solving cue
Modus Ponens P -> Q, P; therefore Q The condition is actually satisfied.
Modus Tollens P -> Q, not Q; therefore not P The consequence failed, so the cause cannot hold.
Transitivity P -> Q, Q -> R; therefore P -> R Two implications form a chain.
Resolution P or Q, not P or R; therefore Q or R A variable appears once positive and once negated.
